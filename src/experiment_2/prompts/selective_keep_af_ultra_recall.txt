You are extracting KEEP atomic facts from a biomedical research article for an expert-written lay summary.

Your task is NOT to extract every article fact. Extract facts that an expert-written lay summary might retain, paraphrase, simplify, or use as explanatory scaffolding for patients, the public, and scientifically curious non-specialists.

Critical risk policy:
- Missing an expert-retained fact is the most dangerous error.
- Over-extracting is acceptable when the fact is plausibly useful for a lay summary.
- Therefore, this is a high-recall KEEP extraction step: include a fact if it could plausibly appear in an expert lay summary as a paraphrase, simplification, generalization, definition, analogy, or background bridge.
- Expert lay summaries, especially eLife-style summaries, often include general educational background before describing the study itself. Do not drop a fact merely because it is not novel.
- Treat article-supported background bridge facts as ordinary KEEP facts. Do not put them in a separate category: if a lay summary might use the fact to open the topic, define a key term, explain a normal biological process, describe a disease/method, or explain why the process matters, include it in keep_atomic_facts.

# KEEP a fact if it is
- A core finding, conclusion, implication, or take-home message of the study.
- Necessary for a lay reader to understand the study's central question, motivation, disease context, biological system, or why the research matters.
- A high-level description of what the researchers did, what they measured, or why a method, model organism, imaging approach, dataset, or experimental system made the study possible.
- A key mechanism, relationship, comparison, direction of effect, or outcome that could explain the study's main story.
- Safety-relevant, clinically relevant, translational, diagnostic, therapeutic, prognostic, or public-health relevant.
- Background that an expert lay summary might need to make the central finding understandable.
- A technical-sounding fact that could be paraphrased into a lay-friendly statement preserving the same core information.
- Introductory background that explains a disease, biological process, organ, cell type, organism, imaging method, measurement approach, or general scientific problem.
- Motivation or problem-setting facts, including what is currently unknown, why existing methods are limited, or why the research question matters.
- Lay-friendly analogies or general-domain background used to explain the study, even if the fact is not itself a new research finding.
- Normal biological processes or baseline mechanisms that an expert summary might mention before explaining what the study discovered.
- Definitions of key concepts that a lay reader would need, such as what a technique measures, what a cell type does, what a molecular process means, or what a kind of damage/disorder is.
- General bridge facts that connect a specialized study to everyday or broader scientific understanding.
- Basic article-supported background that a lay summary might use before the study findings, including normal biological processes, key term definitions, disease or method background, and why the studied process or system matters.

# eLife-style background rescue policy
KEEP these facts when they are present in the source text, even if they are introductory rather than novel:
- What a normal biological process does before explaining what goes wrong.
- What a named molecule, cell type, tissue, organ, organism, disease, imaging technique, or repair pathway does.
- Why a common method is limited, risky, invasive, low-resolution, technically difficult, or insufficient.
- Why the authors chose a model organism, system, dataset, or method, if that reason helps a lay reader understand the study.
- Simple analogies or comparisons used to explain a scientific concept.
- General consequences such as mutations causing disease, DNA damage harming cells, or disrupted blood flow causing harm, when these frame the article's main story.
- Opening-background facts such as DNA replication copying genetic information, sister chromatids separating during cell division, double-strand breaks being serious DNA damage, or imaging methods measuring biological activity, when the source text supports those facts.

# Do NOT extract
- Citation-only, related-work-only, figure/table reference, or intra-paper structural information.
- Routine protocol steps, reagent catalog details, software settings, instrument settings, or statistical procedures.
- Fine-grained quantitative detail, residue-level detail, parameter values, subgroup counts, intermediate measurements, or timing/latency details unlikely to affect the summary's main message.
- Details that are clearly peripheral and cannot reasonably be generalized into a lay-friendly sentence.
- Operational methods details such as cloning constructs, injection volumes, incubation conditions, buffer composition, electrophoresis settings, package names, implementation details, or routine data-processing steps, unless the detail itself is central to the lay explanation.
- Facts whose only likely lay-summary paraphrase would be "the researchers performed a standard technical step."
- Long lists of genes, proteins, variants, parameters, populations, or assay conditions unless the list is itself the main conclusion.

# Precision guidance
- Prefer broad, explanatory facts over procedural minutiae.
- Prefer facts from the title, abstract, introduction, author summary, discussion, and conclusion over late methods details.
- If a methods-section sentence explains WHY a method/model was needed, KEEP the reason.
- If a methods-section sentence only says HOW a routine procedure was done, SKIP it.

# Atomic fact requirements
Each extracted fact must:
1. Contain exactly ONE fact.
2. Be understandable without surrounding context.
3. Resolve pronouns and vague references when possible.
4. Include necessary qualifiers such as study population, organism, disease, intervention, or experimental condition.
5. Never introduce information not present in the source text.
