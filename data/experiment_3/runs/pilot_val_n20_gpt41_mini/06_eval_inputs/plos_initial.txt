Chagas disease is a serious infection caused by the parasite Trypanosoma cruzi, affecting millions of people mainly in Latin America. This disease can cause different health problems, especially heart disease, but it is not fully understood how different types of the parasite relate to the symptoms people develop. In this study, researchers examined blood samples from 65 patients with chronic Chagas disease in Brazil to identify the specific parasite types (called discrete typing units or DTUs) and measure how many parasites were in their blood. They used advanced DNA tests to detect and classify the parasites directly from the patients' blood. About half of the patients had detectable parasite levels, with older patients (over 70 years) showing higher amounts. The most common parasite types found were TcVI and TcII, with some patients infected by more than one type. Interestingly, patients infected with TcII generally had higher parasite levels but were less likely to have heart disease compared to those with TcVI. The study also found that parasite types TcIII and TcV, previously thought to be rare in Brazil, were present in some patients. Overall, the findings improve understanding of the variety and distribution of T. cruzi types in Brazil and suggest that patient age and parasite type influence parasite levels in the blood. This information may help guide future research on disease progression and treatment.
Leishmania infantum is a parasite that causes a serious disease called visceral leishmaniasis, affecting organs like the liver and spleen. Scientists long thought this parasite reproduced mainly by cloning itself, but recent evidence suggests it can also mate and exchange genetic material inside the sand fly that spreads the disease. This study tested whether parasites of the same genetic type can mate within the sand fly. Researchers created two versions of L. infantum that glowed different colors and were resistant to different drugs. When sand flies were fed a mix of these parasites, some offspring showed both colors and drug resistances, proving that mating and genetic exchange occurred between identical strains. These hybrid parasites had three sets of chromosomes (triploid) and remained stable through their life cycle, including infection in mice. Importantly, the hybrid parasites caused less severe infections in mice, with lower parasite numbers and reduced activity of an enzyme linked to disease severity. This work shows that L. infantum can mate within its natural sand fly vector even between identical strains, producing stable but less aggressive hybrids. Understanding this mating process may help explain how the parasite spreads and adapts in nature.
This study explores how a genetic element called Salmonella genomic island 1 (SGI1), which carries multiple antibiotic resistance genes, spreads between bacteria. SGI1 cannot move on its own but relies on helper plasmids called IncC plasmids to transfer between bacterial cells. These IncC plasmids produce a complex structure called a type IV secretion system (T4SS) that forms a channel for DNA transfer during bacterial mating. SGI1 carries three genes similar to parts of this channel, but their role was unclear. The researchers found that SGI1’s genes are activated by the helper plasmid and produce proteins that can replace or mix with the plasmid’s own channel components. This mixing creates a modified channel that allows SGI1 to transfer more efficiently than the helper plasmid itself. Importantly, one SGI1 protein helps it bypass a natural barrier called entry exclusion, which normally prevents DNA transfer into cells already carrying the helper plasmid. This ability lets SGI1 spread more easily within bacterial populations that already have IncC plasmids. These findings reveal that SGI1 not only uses the helper plasmid’s transfer machinery but also customizes it to boost its own spread, helping explain how multidrug resistance genes can rapidly disseminate among harmful bacteria.
This study investigated how the brain of locusts processes smells by transforming complex, synchronized signals from one brain area (the antennal lobe) into simpler, more selective signals in another area called the mushroom body, which is important for memory. Researchers used computer models based on real locust brain data to explore how changes in the strength of connections (synapses) between these two areas help create specific and reliable responses to different odors. They compared two types of learning rules: one based on the exact timing of nerve impulses (spike-timing dependent plasticity, STDP) and another based on the overall rate of nerve firing (spike-rate dependent plasticity, SRDP). Both types of plasticity made the mushroom body neurons respond more sparsely and specifically to odors, meaning fewer neurons responded but with greater precision. However, the timing-based rule (STDP) was better at maintaining distinct responses to multiple odors and improving the ability to tell similar smells apart. The model showed that plasticity fine-tunes the system to detect synchronized input patterns, which is crucial for accurate odor discrimination. These findings suggest that experience-dependent changes in synaptic strength help stabilize and sharpen odor representations in the brain, supporting learning and memory. The locust olfactory system thus provides a useful model for understanding how neural circuits combine timing and plasticity to process sensory information efficiently.
Natural killer (NK) cells are immune cells that help control viral infections by killing infected cells and producing important signaling proteins called cytokines. Varicella zoster virus (VZV), which causes chickenpox and shingles, can infect human NK cells, but it was unclear how this affects their ability to fight infection. This study used laboratory experiments to grow human NK cells with VZV-infected cells and then tested how well the NK cells could kill target cells and produce cytokines. The researchers found that VZV strongly impaired NK cells’ ability to kill infected cells and produce key cytokines, even in NK cells that were not themselves infected but were exposed to the virus. This loss of function required direct contact between NK cells and VZV-infected cells and was not caused by changes in the NK cells’ surface receptors or by substances released into the surrounding fluid. Further analysis showed that VZV disrupted early steps in the NK cell activation process, effectively paralyzing their response to infected targets. A related virus, herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1), was also able to infect and inhibit NK cells, though it affected cytokine production differently. These findings reveal a new way that VZV and HSV-1 evade the immune system by directly blocking NK cell function, which may help the viruses spread and persist in the body. Understanding this interaction could inform future research on viral infections and immune system regulation.
This study explores how the natural variability, or "noise," in the responses of groups of sensory neurons affects their ability to represent information about a stimulus. When the same stimulus is shown repeatedly, neurons respond with some randomness, and this noise can be correlated between pairs of neurons. The researchers asked: how do these noise correlations influence the overall accuracy of the neural population in encoding the stimulus? To answer this, they used a broad mathematical model that makes no assumptions about the specific properties of neurons or their responses. They examined different ways to measure coding accuracy and studied how changing noise correlations impacts these measures. Key findings include: (1) If noise correlations between neuron pairs have opposite signs to their signal correlations (which reflect how similarly neurons respond to changes in the stimulus), then coding accuracy improves compared to when noise is independent. This is called the "sign rule." (2) However, the sign rule is not the only way to improve coding; many diverse noise correlation patterns, including those seen in experiments, can enhance coding even if they violate the sign rule. (3) The best noise correlation patterns always lie on the boundary of the mathematically allowed set of correlations, which is shaped by constraints like fixed individual neuron variability. (4) Under certain conditions, noise correlations can be arranged so that the neural population codes the stimulus as accurately as if there were no noise at all, effectively canceling out noise effects. This noise cancellation is not rare and can occur in large populations. The study also shows that there is a large variety of noise correlation patterns that achieve near-optimal coding, suggesting biological systems might have flexibility in how they organize noise correlations. These results provide a unified understanding of how noise correlations affect neural coding and highlight that optimal coding can arise from many different correlation structures, some of which differ from classical expectations. This work helps clarify why experimentally observed noise correlations sometimes do not follow simple rules and offers a framework for interpreting their role in sensory processing.
Zika virus (ZIKV) is a virus spread by mosquitoes that can cause serious birth defects, including microcephaly, where babies are born with smaller heads and brain problems. While ZIKV outbreaks have been reported in the United States, it was unclear when the virus first affected pregnant women there. This study looked at stored blood samples from 18 mothers in Hawaii who gave birth between 2009 and 2012—six had babies with microcephaly and 12 had healthy babies. Researchers tested the mothers' blood for signs of past or recent ZIKV infection by checking for specific antibodies. They found that some mothers of babies with microcephaly had antibodies indicating ZIKV infection, while most mothers of healthy babies did not. Although the small number of cases meant the results were not statistically definitive, the findings suggest a possible link between ZIKV infection during pregnancy and microcephaly in Hawaii as early as 2009. This timing matches known ZIKV outbreaks in nearby Pacific islands, and it is possible that some mothers were exposed to the virus while traveling. The study highlights the need for larger, future research to better understand ZIKV’s impact on pregnancies in the United States.
This study investigated which bacterial genes help the pathogen Pseudomonas aeruginosa survive and grow in chronic wound infections, which are long-lasting wounds often difficult to heal. Unlike acute infections that develop quickly and cause severe symptoms, chronic infections can persist for months or years with bacteria living in the wound without spreading widely. Researchers collected many bacterial samples from human chronic wounds and found that many bacteria had lost common traits linked to causing damage, such as producing toxins or forming protective biofilms (clusters of bacteria surrounded by a protective matrix). To understand which bacterial functions are truly important in wounds, the team used a mouse model of chronic wounds and tested many bacterial mutants lacking specific genes. They discovered that most traditional virulence factors and biofilm formation genes were not needed for bacteria to survive in wounds. Instead, genes that help bacteria grow without oxygen (anaerobic growth), manage energy and metabolism, and maintain their cell membranes were critical. Further tests showed that the stresses bacteria face in wounds—such as low oxygen, oxidative damage, and membrane stress—can be mimicked by growing bacteria densely in lab conditions without a host. This suggests that bacteria in wounds mainly deal with stresses caused by their own growth environment rather than direct attacks from the host. The findings challenge the idea that targeting biofilm formation or common virulence factors will be effective treatments for chronic wound infections. Instead, therapies that disrupt bacterial metabolism or stress resistance might be more promising. Overall, this work improves understanding of how P. aeruginosa adapts to chronic wounds and highlights new directions for developing treatments.
This study investigated how the DNA of a bacterial virus called phage Mu is organized when it is integrated into the E. coli bacterial chromosome. Normally, bacterial DNA is tightly packed into loops called supercoiled domains, which help protect and regulate the DNA. The researchers found that the entire Mu genome forms its own stable loop, or 'Mu domain,' held together by specific viral DNA ends and a special DNA site in the middle called SGS. This loop structure depends on both viral proteins, especially MuB, and bacterial proteins known as nucleoid-associated proteins (NAPs) that help shape bacterial DNA. Using genetic tools that measure how close different DNA sites are, the team showed that the Mu ends are brought very close together inside this domain, even though they are far apart in the linear DNA sequence. This domain was seen at different places in the bacterial chromosome and was not found for another phage, lambda, suggesting it is unique to Mu. The Mu domain likely keeps the virus ready to move or 'transpose' within the bacterial genome by maintaining the right DNA shape and proximity of its ends. Additionally, low-level activity of certain viral genes within this domain may provide benefits to the host bacteria, helping the virus remain stable over time. Overall, the study reveals a special DNA loop structure formed by phage Mu in bacteria that supports both viral readiness and potentially host advantages.
This study investigated how zebrafish are able to regrow their amputated fins, focusing on the early step where a special layer of skin cells, called the regeneration epidermis, forms and signals to underlying tissues to start regrowth. Researchers used a genetic approach to find mutations that block this process and identified a key gene called lamb1a, which produces a protein important for the structure and function of this skin layer. After fin amputation, lamb1a is turned on in certain skin cells and helps organize them properly, allowing them to send signals needed for bone-forming cells (osteoblasts) to grow and arrange correctly. The study showed that lamb1a is controlled by a growth factor called Fgf20a and that without lamb1a, the skin cells lose their normal organization, signaling is disrupted, and fin regeneration fails. Interestingly, lamb1a is not needed for normal body growth or simple wound healing, highlighting its specific role in regeneration. The findings reveal that lamb1a acts as a crucial link between growth factor signals and the formation of a signaling skin layer that supports fin regrowth. This work improves understanding of how complex tissue regeneration is controlled in animals capable of regrowing limbs and may inform future regenerative medicine research.
