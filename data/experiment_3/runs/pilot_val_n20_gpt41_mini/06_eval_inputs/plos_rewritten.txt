Chagas disease is caused by the parasite Trypanosoma cruzi and affects people in different regions of Brazil. In this study, researchers analyzed blood samples from 65 chronic Chagas disease patients born in various parts of Brazil to identify the types of T. cruzi infecting them and to measure the amount of parasite DNA in their blood. About half of the patients had detectable parasite DNA. The most common parasite types found were TcVI and TcII, with some patients infected by both types. A few patients were also infected with less common types, including TcV and a combination of TcIII and TcVI. Older patients, especially those over 70 years old, tended to have higher levels of parasites in their blood. Patients infected with TcII generally had higher parasite levels but were less likely to have heart disease compared to those infected with TcVI. These findings improve understanding of the diversity and distribution of T. cruzi types in Brazil and show that patient age and parasite type can influence parasite levels in chronic Chagas disease.
Leishmania infantum is a parasite that causes visceral leishmaniasis, a serious disease affecting internal organs such as the liver and spleen. This study shows that L. infantum parasites of the same genetic type can exchange genetic material inside the gut of sand flies, their natural insect vectors. Researchers created two genetically modified versions of L. infantum that each carried different fluorescent markers and drug resistance genes. When sand flies were fed a mixture of these parasites, some offspring inherited both markers and drug resistances, demonstrating genetic exchange between identical strains. These hybrid parasites had three sets of chromosomes (triploid) and remained stable throughout their life cycle, including during infection in mice. Mice infected with the hybrid parasites had much lower parasite levels in their organs and maintained body weights similar to uninfected mice, indicating less severe disease. The reduced disease severity was linked to lower activity of an enzyme called arginase, which is associated with disease progression. This work confirms that L. infantum can mate within its natural sand fly vector even between genetically identical parasites, producing stable hybrids with slower virulence.
This study explores how Salmonella genomic island 1 (SGI1), a genetic element carrying multiple antibiotic resistance genes, spreads between bacteria. SGI1 cannot transfer on its own but relies on helper plasmids called IncC plasmids to move between bacterial cells. These IncC plasmids produce a protein complex known as the type IV secretion system (T4SS), which forms a channel that allows DNA to pass from one bacterium to another during conjugation. SGI1 carries three genes similar to parts of this channel, and their expression is activated by the helper plasmid. The proteins made by SGI1 can replace or combine with the plasmid’s own channel components, creating a modified channel that enables SGI1 to transfer more efficiently than the helper plasmid itself. Two of these SGI1 proteins work together and are both needed for SGI1’s optimal transfer. Additionally, one SGI1 protein helps it bypass a natural barrier called entry exclusion, which normally prevents DNA transfer into cells already carrying the helper plasmid. This ability allows SGI1 to spread more easily within bacterial populations that already have IncC plasmids. Overall, the findings show that SGI1 not only uses the helper plasmid’s transfer machinery but also customizes it to boost its own spread, providing insight into how multidrug resistance genes can rapidly disseminate among harmful bacteria.
This study used computer models based on locust brain data to investigate how the insect olfactory system transforms dense, synchronized signals from antennal lobe projection neurons into sparse and specific responses in mushroom body Kenyon cells, which are involved in memory. Before learning, many Kenyon cells responded to odors but with low reliability and less selectivity. The models compared two types of synaptic plasticity at the connections between antennal lobe projection neurons and Kenyon cells: spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP), which depends on the precise timing of nerve impulses, and spike-rate-dependent plasticity (SRDP), which depends on the overall firing rate. Both plasticity types made Kenyon cell responses sparser and more selective, with fewer neurons responding but more reliably. STDP was more effective at maintaining distinct responses to multiple odors and improving discrimination between similar smells. Plasticity fine-tunes synaptic strengths to enable Kenyon cells to detect synchronized input patterns, which is crucial for accurate and stable odor representation. The findings suggest that experience-dependent changes in synaptic strength help stabilize and sharpen odor coding in the mushroom body, supporting learning and memory. The locust olfactory system thus offers a valuable model for understanding how neural circuits combine timing and plasticity to process sensory information efficiently.
Natural killer (NK) cells are immune cells that help control viral infections by killing infected cells and producing signaling proteins called cytokines. This study found that varicella zoster virus (VZV), the virus that causes chickenpox and shingles, can infect human NK cells and strongly impair their ability to kill target cells and produce cytokines. Importantly, this loss of function occurs not only in NK cells infected by VZV but also in NK cells that are exposed to the virus without becoming infected. The impairment requires direct contact between NK cells and VZV-infected cells and is not caused by changes in NK cell surface receptors or by substances released into the surrounding fluid. Despite this, NK cells remain capable of releasing cytotoxic granules when stimulated in a way that bypasses early activation steps, indicating that VZV blocks NK cell responses at an early stage of activation. A related virus, herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1), can also infect and inhibit NK cells, though it affects cytokine production differently. These findings reveal a new strategy used by VZV and HSV-1 to evade the immune system by directly blocking NK cell function, which may help these viruses spread and persist in the body.
This study explores how the variability, or "noise," in the responses of groups of sensory neurons affects their ability to represent information about a stimulus. When the same stimulus is presented repeatedly, neurons respond with some randomness, and this noise can be correlated between pairs of neurons. Using a broad mathematical framework that does not assume specific neuron properties, the researchers examined how different patterns of noise correlations influence coding accuracy. They considered several measures of coding performance, including linear Fisher information, the optimal linear estimator (OLE) information, and mutual information under Gaussian assumptions. A key finding is the "sign rule": when noise correlations between neuron pairs have opposite signs to their signal correlations—reflecting how similarly neurons respond to stimulus changes—coding accuracy improves compared to independent noise. However, the sign rule is not the only way to enhance coding; many diverse noise correlation patterns, including those observed experimentally, can improve coding even if they violate the sign rule. The best noise correlation patterns always lie on the boundary of the mathematically allowed set of correlations, constrained by the requirement that the noise covariance matrix be positive semidefinite and by fixed individual neuron variances. Importantly, under specific mathematical conditions involving the noise covariance and readout vectors, noise correlations can be arranged to achieve coding performance as good as if there were no noise at all, effectively canceling noise effects. This noise cancellation is not rare and can occur in large neural populations. Moreover, there is a large variety of noise correlation patterns that achieve near-optimal coding, suggesting biological systems may have flexibility in organizing noise correlations. These results provide a unified understanding of how noise correlations affect neural coding and explain why experimentally observed noise correlations sometimes do not follow simple rules. The study highlights that optimal coding can arise from many different correlation structures, some differing from classical expectations, and that the set of optimal noise correlations forms a high-dimensional convex set on the boundary of allowed correlations.
Zika virus (ZIKV) is a virus spread by mosquitoes that has been linked to babies being born with microcephaly, a condition where the baby's head is smaller than normal. This study looked at stored blood samples from 18 mothers in Hawaii who gave birth between 2009 and 2012—six had babies with microcephaly and 12 had healthy babies. Researchers tested the mothers' blood for antibodies that show evidence of ZIKV infection. They found that one mother of a baby with microcephaly had antibodies indicating recent infection, and three mothers had antibodies indicating past infection. Most mothers of healthy babies did not have these antibodies. Although the number of cases was small and the results were not statistically definitive, the findings suggest a possible link between ZIKV infection during pregnancy and microcephaly in Hawaii as early as 2009. This timing overlaps with known ZIKV outbreaks in nearby Pacific islands, so some mothers may have been exposed to the virus while traveling. The study highlights the need for further research to better understand ZIKV’s impact on pregnancies in the United States.
This study investigated which bacterial genes help Pseudomonas aeruginosa survive and grow in chronic wound infections, where bacteria remain localized for months without spreading throughout the body. Researchers collected many bacterial samples from human chronic wounds and found that many bacteria had defects in traits linked to causing damage, such as producing proteases, rhamnolipids, motility, and biofilm formation. Using a mouse model of chronic wounds, they tested bacterial mutants lacking specific genes and found that most traditional virulence factors and biofilm formation genes were not needed for bacteria to survive in wounds. The only virulence function affecting bacterial fitness was the type 3 secretion system. Instead, genes involved in anaerobic growth, metabolism, energy production, and maintaining membrane integrity were critical. Further tests showed that bacteria face stresses in wounds such as low oxygen, oxidative damage, and membrane stress. These stresses could be mimicked by growing bacteria densely in laboratory conditions without a host, suggesting that bacteria in wounds mainly deal with stresses from their own growth environment rather than direct attacks from the host. The findings challenge the idea that targeting biofilm formation or common virulence factors will be effective treatments for chronic wound infections. Instead, therapies that disrupt bacterial metabolism or stress resistance might be more promising. Overall, this work improves understanding of how P. aeruginosa adapts to chronic wounds and highlights new directions for developing treatments.
This study investigated how the DNA of phage Mu, a virus that integrates into the E. coli bacterial chromosome, is organized when it is in its dormant prophage state. The researchers found that the entire Mu genome forms a stable loop structure, called the Mu domain, which brings the two ends of the Mu DNA close together despite being far apart in the linear sequence. This loop depends on a special DNA site near the center of Mu called the SGS site, the Mu L end, and the MuB protein produced by the phage. Host bacterial proteins known as nucleoid-associated proteins (NAPs) also help maintain this domain. The Mu domain was observed at different locations in the bacterial chromosome and was not found for another phage, lambda, indicating it is unique to Mu. Low-level transcription from two phage promoters supports the formation and maintenance of the Mu domain, with one promoter’s activity depending on the domain structure. The Mu domain likely keeps the prophage ready for transposition by maintaining the proper DNA shape and proximity of its ends. Additionally, some genes expressed at low levels within this domain may provide benefits to the host bacterium, helping the prophage remain stable over time. Overall, the study reveals a special DNA loop formed by phage Mu in bacteria that supports both viral readiness and potentially host advantages.
This study investigated how zebrafish regenerate their amputated fins, focusing on the role of a gene called lamb1a. After fin injury, lamb1a is turned on in a specific layer of skin cells, where it helps organize these cells properly. This organization is important for the skin cells to send signals that guide bone-forming cells to grow and arrange correctly, enabling fin regrowth. The activity of lamb1a depends on a growth factor called Fgf20a. Without lamb1a, the skin cells lose their normal structure, signaling is disrupted, and fin regeneration is blocked. Notably, lamb1a is not needed for normal body growth or simple wound healing, showing its specific role in regeneration. These findings reveal that lamb1a connects growth factor signals to the formation of a signaling skin layer essential for fin regrowth, enhancing our understanding of tissue regeneration in animals capable of regrowing limbs.
