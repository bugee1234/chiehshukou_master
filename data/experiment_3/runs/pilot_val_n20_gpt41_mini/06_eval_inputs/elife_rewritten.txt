This study focuses on Plasmodium vivax malaria, which can cause repeated infections called relapses due to dormant parasites known as hypnozoites in the liver. These hypnozoites can activate weeks to months after the initial infection, leading to new blood-stage infections. The researchers developed a mathematical model describing how hypnozoites behave within the liver and validated it using data from tropical strains of P. vivax. Their model shows that the number of hypnozoites varies widely among individuals, with most carrying few or none and some having many, which leads to more relapses and a higher chance of spreading malaria. The model also predicts that standard malaria control methods, such as mosquito control and treatment of blood infections, reduce transmission but may be less effective against P. vivax because of relapses from hypnozoites. Importantly, adding drugs like primaquine that kill hypnozoites in the liver to treatment plans can greatly reduce relapses and transmission. This is because people with many hypnozoites are more likely to relapse and thus receive treatment that clears both blood and liver infections. These findings highlight the importance of targeting the hidden liver stage of P. vivax to improve malaria control and elimination efforts.
Piezo1 ion channels are proteins in cell membranes that detect mechanical forces by opening in response to tension, allowing ions to flow and create signals. This study used cell-attached patch-clamp electrophysiology to measure the number and behavior of Piezo1 channels in small patches of membrane from cells expressing natural (low) and artificially increased (high) levels of Piezo1. The researchers also used computer simulations to explore how these channels might be arranged on the membrane. Their results showed that at both natural and high channel densities, Piezo1 channels act independently without changing their sensitivity to pressure or their likelihood of opening. This suggests that Piezo1 channels are either spread out randomly or only weakly grouped in small clusters, enabling cells to sense mechanical forces evenly across their surface. The findings indicate that any strong clustering of Piezo1 channels would require additional cellular mechanisms beyond the channels themselves.
This study investigates the role of a protein called Shugoshin-like protein 2 (Sgol2) in ensuring accurate chromosome separation during the first division of egg cells (meiosis I) in mammals. Sgol2 protects the connections between sister chromatids by recruiting the enzyme PP2A to centromeres, which is essential for maintaining cohesion during this division. Additionally, Sgol2 helps turn off the spindle assembly checkpoint (SAC), a safety mechanism that delays cell division until chromosomes are properly attached, by binding directly to the checkpoint protein Mad2. Sgol2 also recruits the protein MCAK to kinetochores, which helps limit excessive stretching of chromosome pairs. Furthermore, Sgol2 promotes the formation of stable microtubule attachments by inhibiting the activity of Aurora B/C kinases at kinetochores. These functions depend on different parts of the Sgol2 protein and specific modifications, showing that Sgol2 acts as a central coordinator. Together, these findings reveal that Sgol2 integrates multiple mechanisms to ensure chromosomes are correctly attached and segregated during meiosis I, highlighting its essential role in preventing chromosome segregation errors in egg cells.
Plants use autophagy, a process that helps protect them from infections by breaking down unwanted components inside their cells. This process involves proteins like ATG8CL and cargo receptors such as Joka2, which help target specific materials for degradation and support the plant's defense against pathogens. The study examined how the potato blight pathogen Phytophthora infestans interferes with this defense system. Researchers found that the pathogen produces a protein called PexRD54 that binds strongly to the plant's ATG8CL protein and increases the formation of autophagosomes—structures that carry materials to be broken down. However, PexRD54 also displaces the plant's cargo receptor Joka2 from ATG8CL, weakening the plant's resistance. Experiments showed that increasing Joka2 levels reduced disease symptoms, but this protective effect was blocked when PexRD54 was present. Overall, the study reveals that PexRD54 hijacks the plant's autophagy machinery to promote infection by outcompeting a defense-related protein, highlighting a sophisticated way pathogens can undermine plant immunity.
This study investigated the roles of two related proteins, TBP and TBP2, in activating muscle genes during muscle cell development and repair. The Pre-Initiation Complex (PIC), which initiates gene activity, includes TBP as a key component. Earlier research suggested that during muscle formation, TBP might be replaced by TBP2 to activate muscle genes. However, this study found that TBP2 is not present in muscle cells or muscle stem cells during differentiation, and mice lacking TBP2 show normal muscle repair after injury. Instead, TBP and other parts of the TFIID complex remain present, though at reduced levels, and are necessary for activating muscle genes. When TBP was experimentally reduced in muscle cells, these cells failed to develop properly into muscle fibers, showing that TBP is essential for muscle gene activation. Overall, the findings demonstrate that TBP, not TBP2, is the critical protein for muscle gene expression during muscle cell differentiation and regeneration.
This study investigated the protein PALB2, which works with BRCA1 and BRCA2—proteins linked to breast cancer and Fanconi anemia—to help repair damaged DNA. PALB2 acts as a scaffold connecting these proteins and also binds directly to DNA, although the role of this DNA binding was not well understood before. The researchers identified a key DNA-binding region in PALB2 and showed that mutations in just four amino acids within this region reduce the cell’s ability to repair DNA damage by about half. They also found that this part of PALB2 can promote important DNA repair activities on its own, even without other proteins usually involved in the process. Additionally, PALB2 can work with RNA as well as DNA, suggesting it might have broader roles in DNA repair linked to gene activity. These findings highlight that PALB2’s ability to bind DNA is essential for effective repair of damaged chromosomes, which helps protect cells from becoming cancerous. Understanding how PALB2 functions could improve knowledge of cancer development and guide future research on DNA repair mechanisms.
This study explored how the electrical charge on protein surfaces affects their movement inside the cytoplasm of three microorganisms: Escherichia coli, Lactococcus lactis, and Haloferax volcanii. Using green fluorescent proteins (GFPs) engineered to have different net charges, the researchers found that in E. coli, proteins with positive charges moved much more slowly—up to 100 times slower—than negatively charged or neutral proteins. This slowdown is mainly caused by positively charged proteins binding to ribosomes, the cell's protein-making machines. In Lactococcus lactis and Haloferax volcanii, the effect of positive charge on slowing diffusion was less pronounced, likely because their higher internal salt concentrations reduce these electrostatic interactions. Additionally, how positive charges are spread over the protein surface influences diffusion speed, with uneven charge distribution causing slower movement. When cells experienced increased crowding, protein diffusion slowed further. Analysis of the natural proteins in these organisms showed that most cytoplasmic proteins carry negative charges, which may help them avoid slow movement caused by binding to ribosomes. These findings suggest that ribosome surface properties influence which proteins can move efficiently inside cells, affecting overall protein mobility and cellular function.
This study investigated how nerve cells recycle synaptic vesicles (SVs), which are small sacs that store and release chemical messengers for brain communication. When nerve cells are strongly stimulated, SVs release their contents and their membranes are rapidly taken back into the cell through a process called bulk endocytosis, forming large structures known as bulk endosomes. These bulk endosomes then serve as sources for generating new SVs. The researchers studied nerve cells from mice lacking two proteins, dynamin 1 and dynamin 3, which are important for a common recycling method called clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME). They found that bulk endocytosis still occurred normally or was even increased without these proteins, and new SVs were still formed from bulk endosomes, although this reformation was slower. Detailed microscopy showed that clathrin-coated pits appeared only on the cell surface and not on bulk endosomes, and removing clathrin did not further reduce SV recovery. These findings reveal that during intense nerve activity, nerve cells use a recycling pathway that does not require dynamin 1, dynamin 3, or clathrin to regenerate SVs from bulk endosomes. This improves our understanding of how nerve cells maintain communication under high demand and highlights alternative mechanisms for vesicle recycling beyond the well-known clathrin-dependent pathway.
Macropinocytosis is a process used by cells, including amoebae like Dictyostelium, to take in large amounts of fluid from their environment, which helps them gather nutrients and other molecules. This process involves the cell membrane forming cup-shaped structures called macropinocytic cups, whose walls are pushed outward by actin protein filaments. Researchers found that these cups form around patches of signaling molecules such as PIP3, active Ras, and active Rac. Around these patches, a ring of active SCAR/WAVE protein is recruited to the edges, which promotes actin polymerization and helps build the cup walls. The formation of these signaling patches does not depend on the surrounding actin ring, and increasing Ras activity leads to larger patches and cups. Most new macropinocytic cups form by splitting from existing ones rather than by folding of membrane ruffles. Similar patterns of signaling and SCAR rings are also seen in related structures like phagocytic cups and basal actin waves. These findings suggest that macropinocytic cups arise through a self-organizing process where patches of active Ras, PIP3, and Rac recruit SCAR to their edges, driving actin ring formation and cup extension. This work improves our understanding of how cells control the shape and formation of macropinocytic cups, a key step in fluid uptake important for cell nutrition and immune function.
During early development, cells divide rapidly without growing, causing them to become smaller with each division. The mitotic spindle, a structure made of microtubules, separates chromosomes during cell division and must adjust its size to fit the shrinking cells. This study used extracts from frog (Xenopus laevis) embryos at different developmental stages to recreate spindle formation in the lab. They found that spindle size decreases as cells get smaller, mainly because of increased activity of a protein called kif2a that destabilizes microtubules. In early large cells, kif2a is kept inactive by another protein, importin α, which binds to kif2a and prevents it from shrinking the spindle. As development proceeds and cells become smaller, importin α moves to the cell membrane, reducing its availability in the cytoplasm and allowing kif2a to become active and reduce spindle size. Changing spindle size in embryos affected how the spindle oriented itself during metaphase but did not disrupt chromosome separation. This work shows that spindle size is controlled by a balance between kif2a and importin α, linked to cell size through the redistribution of importin α between the cytoplasm and membrane. Understanding this mechanism helps explain how cells coordinate internal structures with changing cell size during development.
