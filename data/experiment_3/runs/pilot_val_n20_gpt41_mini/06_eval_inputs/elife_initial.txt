This study focuses on Plasmodium vivax malaria, a common type of malaria that can cause repeated infections called relapses. These relapses happen because the parasite can hide in the liver in a dormant form called hypnozoites, which can activate weeks or months after the initial infection and cause new blood infections. The researchers developed a mathematical model to understand how these dormant parasites behave inside the liver and how they contribute to malaria transmission. They used data from tropical regions to test their model and found that people carry very different numbers of hypnozoites—most have few or none, but some have many, leading to more relapses and greater chances of spreading malaria. The model also showed that standard malaria control methods like mosquito control and treating blood infections reduce transmission, but relapses from hypnozoites make P. vivax harder to control than other malaria types. Importantly, the study predicts that adding drugs like primaquine, which kill hypnozoites in the liver, to treatment plans can greatly reduce relapses and transmission. This is because people with many hypnozoites are more likely to relapse and thus receive treatment that clears both blood and liver infections. The findings highlight the need to target the hidden liver stage of P. vivax to better control and eventually eliminate this form of malaria.
Cells sense mechanical forces through special proteins called Piezo1 ion channels, which open in response to membrane tension and allow ions to flow, creating signals. This study investigated whether Piezo1 channels influence each other’s activity when they are close together in the cell membrane. Using a technique called patch-clamp electrophysiology, researchers measured the number and behavior of Piezo1 channels in small patches of cell membrane from cells with natural (low) and artificially increased (high) levels of Piezo1. They also used computer simulations to model how these channels might be arranged on the membrane. The key finding was that, at both natural and very high densities, Piezo1 channels act independently without affecting each other’s sensitivity to pressure or likelihood of opening. This suggests that Piezo1 channels are spread out or only weakly clustered, allowing cells to detect mechanical forces evenly across their surface. These results help explain how cells maintain consistent mechanical sensing and indicate that any strong clustering of Piezo1 channels would require additional cellular mechanisms beyond the channels themselves.
This study investigates how a protein called Shugoshin-like protein 2 (Sgol2) helps ensure accurate chromosome separation during the first division of egg cells (meiosis I) in mammals. Proper chromosome separation is crucial because errors can lead to eggs with the wrong number of chromosomes, a common cause of miscarriage and developmental problems. The researchers focused on how Sgol2 coordinates several key processes: protecting the connections between sister chromatids (cohesion), regulating the spindle assembly checkpoint (SAC) that monitors chromosome attachment, promoting correct chromosome alignment and attachment to spindle fibers (kinetochore-microtubule interactions), and controlling the activity of enzymes called Aurora B/C kinases that modify these attachments. Using mouse egg cells lacking Sgol2 or carrying specific mutations, combined with live imaging and molecular techniques, the team found that Sgol2 protects cohesion by recruiting an enzyme called PP2A to centromeres. Sgol2 also helps turn off the SAC by directly binding to a checkpoint protein Mad2, allowing the cell cycle to progress. Additionally, Sgol2 recruits another protein, MCAK, to kinetochores to limit excessive stretching of chromosome pairs and promotes the formation of stable microtubule attachments by inhibiting Aurora B/C kinase activity at kinetochores. Notably, Sgol2’s ability to regulate these processes depends on different parts of the protein and specific modifications, showing it acts as a central coordinator. These findings reveal that Sgol2 integrates multiple mechanisms to ensure chromosomes are correctly attached and segregated during meiosis I, highlighting its essential role in preventing chromosome segregation errors in egg cells.
Plants use a process called autophagy to protect themselves from infections by breaking down unwanted or harmful components inside their cells. This process involves special proteins, including ATG8 and cargo receptors like Joka2, which help target specific materials for destruction. The study focused on how the potato blight pathogen, Phytophthora infestans, interferes with this defense system. Researchers found that the pathogen produces a protein called PexRD54 that binds strongly to the plant's ATG8CL protein, a key player in autophagy. PexRD54 increases the formation of autophagosomes—structures that carry materials to be degraded—but at the same time, it displaces the plant's cargo receptor Joka2 from ATG8CL. Since Joka2 helps defend the plant by promoting selective autophagy against the pathogen, its removal weakens the plant's defense. Experiments showed that increasing Joka2 levels reduced disease symptoms, but this protective effect was blocked when PexRD54 was present. Overall, the study reveals that PexRD54 hijacks the plant's autophagy machinery to promote infection by outcompeting a defense-related protein, highlighting a sophisticated way pathogens can undermine plant immunity.
This study investigated how certain proteins control the activation of muscle-specific genes during muscle cell development and repair. Normally, gene activity starts when a group of proteins called the Pre-Initiation Complex (PIC) assembles at gene starting points. A key part of this complex is the TFIID protein, which includes TBP (TATA-box binding protein). Previous research suggested that during muscle formation, TBP might be replaced by a related protein called TBP2 (also known as TRF3), which would then activate muscle genes. However, mice lacking TBP2 showed no muscle problems, raising questions about TBP2's role. The researchers tested whether TBP2 is needed for muscle repair in adult mice and found that mice without TBP2 could regenerate muscle normally after injury. They also found that TBP2 is not present in muscle cells or muscle stem cells during differentiation. Instead, TBP and other TFIID components, although reduced in amount, remain present and form complexes essential for turning on muscle genes. When TBP was experimentally reduced in muscle cells, these cells failed to properly develop into muscle fibers, showing that TBP is necessary for muscle gene activation. The study concludes that TBP, not TBP2, is the critical protein for muscle gene expression during muscle cell differentiation and regeneration. This finding clarifies the molecular machinery behind muscle formation and may help understand muscle diseases linked to these proteins.
This study investigated the role of a protein called PALB2, which works with other important proteins (BRCA1 and BRCA2) to repair damaged DNA and prevent cancer. DNA damage repair is crucial because it helps maintain the stability of our chromosomes and prevents mutations that can lead to diseases like breast cancer. PALB2 helps coordinate the repair process by interacting with DNA and other proteins involved in fixing breaks in DNA strands. The researchers identified a specific part of PALB2 that binds directly to DNA and found that changing just four key building blocks (amino acids) in this region reduced the cell’s ability to repair DNA damage by about half. They also discovered that this part of PALB2 can promote important DNA repair steps on its own, even without other proteins usually required for the process. Surprisingly, PALB2 could also work with RNA, suggesting it might have additional roles in DNA repair linked to gene activity. These findings highlight that PALB2’s ability to bind DNA is essential for effective repair of damaged chromosomes, which helps protect cells from becoming cancerous. Understanding how PALB2 works could improve knowledge of cancer development and potentially guide future treatments targeting DNA repair pathways.
This study explored how the movement of proteins inside cells is affected by their electrical charge and the saltiness (ionic strength) of the cell fluid. Proteins need to move around inside cells to interact and carry out important functions, especially in bacteria where active transport is limited. The researchers used specially designed glowing proteins with different surface charges, from very negative to very positive, and measured how fast they moved inside three types of microorganisms: Escherichia coli, Lactococcus lactis, and Haloferax volcanii. They found that in E. coli, proteins with a positive charge moved much more slowly—up to 100 times slower—than negatively charged ones. This slowdown was mainly because positively charged proteins stick to ribosomes, the cell's protein-making machines, which slows their movement. In the other two organisms, this effect was less strong due to higher salt levels that reduce these sticky interactions. The study also showed that how the positive charges are spread on the protein surface matters for movement speed. When cells were exposed to conditions that increase crowding inside the cell, protein movement slowed even more. By analyzing the proteins naturally found in these organisms, the researchers found that most cytoplasmic proteins are negatively charged, likely to avoid slow movement caused by sticking to ribosomes. Interestingly, some bacteria that live inside other cells have mostly positively charged proteins, which raises questions about how they manage protein movement. Overall, the findings suggest that the electrical charge of proteins and their interactions with ribosomes play an important role in controlling how proteins move inside cells, which can influence cell function and protein production.
This study investigated how nerve cells recycle synaptic vesicles (SVs), which are tiny sacs that store and release chemical messengers needed for brain communication. When nerve cells are strongly stimulated, SVs release their contents and their membranes are quickly taken back into the cell through a process called bulk endocytosis, forming large structures called bulk endosomes. These bulk endosomes then help create new SVs, but the exact steps and proteins involved were unclear. The researchers studied nerve cells from mice genetically modified to lack two important proteins, dynamin 1 and dynamin 3, which are usually critical for a common recycling method called clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME). They found that bulk endocytosis still occurred normally or even more than usual without these proteins, and new SVs were still formed from bulk endosomes, although more slowly. Importantly, this SV reformation did not depend on clathrin, another protein thought to be essential for vesicle recycling. Using advanced microscopy and labeling techniques, the team showed that clathrin-coated structures appeared only on the cell surface, not on bulk endosomes, and removing clathrin did not further block SV recovery. These results suggest that during intense nerve activity, nerve cells use a recycling pathway that does not require dynamin 1, dynamin 3, or clathrin to regenerate SVs from bulk endosomes. This finding improves our understanding of how nerve cells maintain communication under high demand and points to alternative mechanisms for vesicle recycling beyond the well-known clathrin-dependent pathway.
Macropinocytosis is a process cells use to take in large amounts of fluid from their surroundings, which helps them gather nutrients and other important molecules. This process involves the cell membrane forming cup-shaped structures called macropinocytic cups, which are pushed outward by a ring of actin protein filaments. However, how cells organize actin into these rings has been unclear. This study used advanced 3D microscopy to closely observe macropinocytosis in a model organism called Dictyostelium. The researchers found that macropinocytic cups form around patches of active signaling molecules, including Ras and PIP3, which appear in the center of the cup. Surrounding these patches is a ring of active SCAR/WAVE protein, which promotes actin polymerization and helps build the cup walls. Importantly, these signaling patches form independently of the actin ring and can grow larger when Ras activity is increased. Most new macropinocytic cups form by splitting from existing ones rather than by folding of membrane ruffles. Similar patterns of signaling and SCAR rings were also seen in related structures like phagocytic cups and basal actin waves. The findings suggest that macropinocytic cups arise through a self-organizing process where patches of active Ras and PIP3 recruit SCAR to their edges, which then drives actin ring formation and cup extension. This new understanding clarifies how cells control the shape and formation of macropinocytic cups, a key step in fluid uptake important for nutrition, immune function, and disease processes.
During early development, animal cells divide rapidly without growing, causing cells to get smaller with each division. The mitotic spindle, a structure made of protein fibers called microtubules, helps separate chromosomes during cell division and must adjust its size to fit the shrinking cells. This study investigated how spindle size changes during development in the frog Xenopus laevis. Researchers created a laboratory system using extracts from embryos at different stages to mimic spindle formation and size seen in living embryos. They found that spindle size decreases as cells get smaller, mainly because of increased activity of a protein called kif2a that destabilizes microtubules. In early large cells, kif2a is kept inactive by another protein, importin α, which binds to kif2a and prevents it from shrinking the spindle. As development proceeds and cells become smaller, importin α moves to the cell membrane, reducing its availability in the cytoplasm and allowing kif2a to become active and reduce spindle size. Changing spindle size in embryos affected how the spindle oriented itself during cell division but did not disrupt chromosome separation. This work shows that spindle size is controlled by a balance between kif2a and importin α, linked to cell size through the distribution of importin α between the cytoplasm and membrane. Understanding this mechanism helps explain how cells coordinate internal structures with changing cell size during development.
