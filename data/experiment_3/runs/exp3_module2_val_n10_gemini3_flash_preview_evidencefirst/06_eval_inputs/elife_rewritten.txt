Cell-fate reprogramming is a fundamental process in biological development where one specific cell type is converted into another. Researchers used C. elegans, a species of roundworm, as the model organism for this study. In the C. elegans germline, converting germ cells into somatic cells requires the disruption of chromatin, which is the material that packages DNA.
Klebsiella pneumoniae is a significant clinical pathogen that causes serious infections in the lungs, blood, liver, and bladder. This bacterium is a leading cause of hospital-associated infections and is becoming increasingly difficult to treat due to the rise of antibiotic-resistant and hypervirulent strains. To investigate how the immune system responds to this threat, researchers used a mouse model to study lung infections caused by a specific hypervirulent strain of the bacteria.

Neutrophils serve as the initial immune cell type to react when Klebsiella pneumoniae pathogens invade the body. These first responders use several mechanisms to kill bacteria, including the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS)—germ-killing chemicals that destroy pathogens. The study found that the adaptor protein SKAP2 is necessary for the immune system to protect against these pulmonary infections. Mice lacking the Skap2 gene had a 100-fold higher bacterial burden in their lungs compared to normal mice. Experiments revealed that SKAP2-expressing neutrophils are the primary cells responsible for containing the bacteria, and they perform this role independently of other immune cells like inflammatory monocytes.

To understand the underlying mechanisms, the team quantified the production of reactive oxygen species in neutrophils after the cells were exposed to Klebsiella pneumoniae. The study discovered that SKAP2 is essential for neutrophils to produce ROS when they are stimulated by the bacteria or through specific integrin receptors. Without SKAP2, the ROS response to Klebsiella pneumoniae was completely abolished. Additionally, the production of these chemicals is partially dependent on SKAP2 when neutrophils are stimulated by immune complexes.

Mechanistically, SKAP2 facilitates the activation of a specific internal signaling circuit involving enzymes such as SFKs, Syk, and Pyk2. Without SKAP2, this circuit cannot fully activate, and the neutrophils fail to produce the protective ROS needed to kill the bacteria. Interestingly, while ROS production was lost, other functions like degranulation remained normal, suggesting SKAP2 specifically regulates the ROS pathway. These findings indicate that SKAP2 expression in immune cells derived from bone marrow is essential for containing bacterial growth. While these results clarify the immune response in mice, it is currently unknown if SKAP2 plays the same role in the human response to Klebsiella pneumoniae.
Plasmodium vivax is the most geographically widespread species of malaria, causing between 80 and 300 million clinical cases annually. Unlike other forms of malaria, P. vivax can hide in the liver as dormant parasites called hypnozoites, making it harder to control than Plasmodium falciparum. These hypnozoites act as a third parasite reservoir alongside the blood and mosquito reservoirs found in other malaria species. Relapses occur when these dormant liver-stage parasites activate and cause new blood-stage infections weeks or months after the initial mosquito bite.

Standard malaria models for P. falciparum are not applicable to P. vivax because they fail to account for this liver reservoir. Researchers developed a mathematical model to simulate the transmission of P. vivax between humans and mosquitoes by accounting for both new bites and relapses. Using individual-level data from patients, the model was employed to estimate the duration until a patient's initial disease relapse. A key assumption in this model is that hypnozoites activate at a constant rate. This suggests that P. vivax relapses may happen right after the primary infection, though they are often masked by the protective effects of anti-malarial drugs.

In the model, the number of hypnozoites in the liver increases with new infections and decreases when they activate or die. For temperate strains, the model includes a long-latency phase where hypnozoites remain dormant for approximately six months before they can activate. The distribution of these dormant parasites is uneven across a population; some individuals carry many parasites while others carry none. A higher number of hypnozoites is associated with more frequent relapses and a shorter time until the first relapse occurs. Individuals with a higher number of hypnozoites are predicted to contribute more to the spread of P. vivax because they relapse more often.

Because a single mosquito bite can cause multiple blood-stage infections through relapses, P. vivax is more difficult to eliminate than P. falciparum. Standard anti-malarial drugs target the parasite in the blood but do not directly target the liver-stage hypnozoites. Primaquine is a specific drug that can eliminate P. vivax hypnozoites from the liver. Including primaquine in first-line treatments is predicted to significantly reduce transmission. Health systems can more efficiently target individuals with the highest hypnozoite counts because they are the most prone to experiencing relapses, making them more likely to be detected and treated. Treating P. vivax with a combination of blood-stage anti-malarials and primaquine can clear both active blood infections and dormant liver-stage parasites, potentially turning the parasite's greatest strength into its Achilles' heel.
Unlike land that is cleared for farming or ranching, forests that are selectively logged for specific trees remain as forested areas and have the potential to regain their stored carbon over time. The recovery of this carbon in logged Amazonian forests happens through two main processes: the continued growth of trees that survived the logging and the arrival of new young trees. Researchers used advanced statistical tools called Bayesian hierarchical models to estimate how these carbon stocks change across the Amazon basin after logging disturbances. The study specifically modeled the recovery of carbon by distinguishing between the growth of surviving trees and the recruitment of new ones. To understand what drives these changes, the researchers investigated how factors like logging intensity, initial forest carbon levels, rainfall, and soil density influence the recovery process. Because many of the study sites had been monitored for less than a decade, the researchers focused their basin-wide predictions on the first 10 years following a disturbance. Across the entire Amazon region, trees that survive the initial logging are the primary contributors to the recovery of carbon stocks. Immediately after logging, the recruitment of new trees is high because there is less competition for sunlight and resources, though this rate slows down as the forest fills back in. The speed at which these surviving trees recover carbon is heavily influenced by how intense the initial logging disturbance was. Carbon recovery over 10 years is significantly higher in the Guiana Shield and western Amazon than in the southern Amazon. In the Guiana Shield and northwestern Amazonia, the high levels of recovery are driven specifically by the robust growth of the surviving trees. In contrast, the southern Amazon shows the lowest rates of carbon recovery compared to other regions. High environmental stress in the south, characterized by low rainfall and high seasonality, limits the ability of these forests to bounce back. Specifically, high seasonal water stress in the southern region appears to be the main constraint that slows down the growth of surviving trees. Environmental factors such as annual rainfall, seasonal water stress, and soil density are critical in determining how quickly different forest plots recover. Because many commercial tree species grow slowly, future timber stocks in the Amazon will depend almost entirely on the trees that survive the first round of logging. Ultimately, surviving trees play the most vital role in ensuring that disturbed Amazonian forests can continue to function and store carbon.
For years, neuroscientists have used a laboratory test called the 'neurosphere' assay to study brain stem cells. In this test, researchers observe whether a single cell can grow into a cluster of new neurons and support cells. However, it has been unclear whether the cells that start these clusters in a dish are the same as the true neural stem cells (NSCs) that maintain the brain throughout life.

In the adult mammalian brain, NSCs reside in two specific regions: the subgranular zone and the subventricular zone (SVZ). These cells persist throughout adult life and are responsible for generating new neurons in the olfactory bulb, as well as astrocytes and oligodendrocytes (the brain's support cells) in various brain regions. In their natural environment, these NSCs are typically quiescent, meaning they are in an inactive or 'sleeping' state, yet they retain the power to regenerate the brain after injury.

To better understand these cells, researchers screened 383 antibodies against hundreds of cell surface antigens. From this screen, they pinpointed 17 specific cell surface markers that facilitate the enrichment and isolation of neurosphere-initiating cells (NICs) from the SVZ of adult mice. By combining these markers, the study identified two distinct cell populations: GEPCOT cells and pre-GEPCOT cells.

GEPCOT cells are characterized by high levels of the proteins EGFR and PlexinB2. These cells are highly active and divide frequently, with nearly all of them dividing within a 24-hour period. While GEPCOT cells are excellent at forming neurospheres in laboratory cultures, they are short-lived in the actual brain. In contrast, pre-GEPCOT cells are the true quiescent neural stem cells (qNSCs). They are highly inactive in the living brain and, surprisingly, cannot form neurospheres when placed in standard laboratory cultures. However, they are long-lived and survive treatments like temozolomide (a chemotherapy-like drug) that kill off actively dividing cells. Once the active cells are cleared, the pre-GEPCOT cells 'wake up' to replenish the GEPCOT population and regenerate the SVZ.

The researchers confirmed this relationship using genetic markers. They found that genetic tools (such as GFAP-CreERT2) that provide long-term contributions to the brain specifically label pre-GEPCOT cells. These GFAP-expressing pre-GEPCOT cells contain the qNSCs capable of producing new GEPCOTs, neuroblasts, and various other essential brain cells. This discovery allows scientists to move beyond artificial laboratory cultures and study how true, quiescent stem cells behave and regenerate within a living organism.
