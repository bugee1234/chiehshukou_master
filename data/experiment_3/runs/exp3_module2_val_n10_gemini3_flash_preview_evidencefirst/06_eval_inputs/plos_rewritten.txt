Gene expression levels vary significantly between individuals, influencing traits related to evolution and disease. While most research focuses on how genes start, this study highlights the importance of how they end. Researchers used mRNA sequencing across six individuals to detect single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) specifically located within the 3′ untranslated regions (3′ UTRs) of genes. They also developed a high-throughput approach called 3′-end RNA-seq to precisely measure transcript ends. 

They found that transcript end positions are non-randomly distributed and tend to flank specific regulatory elements, such as microRNA sites. The specific length of a transcript dictates the inclusion of these sites, which are targeted by microRNAs or proteins to mediate gene repression. In immune-related genes, this process—called alternative polyadenylation—is frequently used to include or exclude these motifs. Genetic variants in polyadenylation signals or specific 3′ regulatory motifs, including AREs and Pumilio sites, serve as significant predictors of gene expression levels across individuals. These variations are linked to susceptibility for both rare Mendelian conditions and common complex disorders. These findings suggest that models of human genetic variation are incomplete if they ignore 3′-end processing in favor of transcription initiation and splicing.
The live attenuated simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV) vaccine, SIVΔnef, is one of the most effective tools for inducing protection against wild-type lentiviral infections. While safety concerns prevent its use in humans, it serves as a gold standard for understanding vaccine-mediated immunity. Research in rhesus macaques shows that SIVΔnef provides robust protection against both mucosal challenges and heterologous viral strains.

Using next-generation sequencing to analyze the evolution of the virus in 12 rhesus macaques, researchers found that SIVΔnef undergoes significant sequence evolution during the vaccination period, even when viral levels are nearly undetectable. This evolution triggers a shift in the CD8 T cell immune response between 5 and 20 weeks post-vaccination. Specifically, viral escape from initial immune pressure leads to 'anentropic specificity,' where the immune response shifts to target highly conserved, stable regions of the virus like Gag and Pol. While immune responses directed at these conserved regions remain stable over time, those targeting variable regions diminish as viral mutations occur. Remarkably, this shift occurs in all animals regardless of their genetic background or MHC I genotypes.

As the virus mutates, the immune system maintains responses to stable regions while also generating new responses to viral variants, a process that 'deepens' the immune repertoire. This combination of anentropic specificity and increased response depth correlates with the vaccine's high efficacy. These findings suggest that future HIV vaccines should be designed to focus T cells on conserved viral regions and their variants to achieve similar protection.
When the fungus Candida albicans invades the tongue, the body must rapidly recruit white blood cells called neutrophils to the oral mucosa to prevent the infection from spreading. Using a mouse model of oral candidiasis (OPC), researchers found that the interleukin-1 receptor (IL-1R) pathway is the primary controller of this response. 

When live fungal hyphae damage tongue cells called keratinocytes, these cells release IL-1α as an 'alarmin.' This signal triggers a complex communication loop. Because the fungus does not directly contact underlying blood vessels, the IL-1α signal acts on endothelial cells in the vessels to produce G-CSF. G-CSF is a critical protein that regulates the production and mobilization of neutrophils in the bone marrow. Because of its ability to boost white blood cell levels, G-CSF is already used therapeutically to treat patients with low white blood cell counts (neutropenia).

During infection, both IL-1α and IL-1β are significantly upregulated, facilitating the systemic release of G-CSF into the bloodstream. This growth factor reaches the bone marrow to trigger 'emergency granulopoiesis,' producing a fresh supply of neutrophils to meet the high demand. Simultaneously, IL-1R signaling stimulates keratinocytes to produce chemokines that recruit existing neutrophils from the blood to the infection site. These findings highlight how the body translates a local fungal attack into a coordinated systemic immune response.
Lymphatic filariasis is a parasitic infection that stands as a leading cause of permanent and long-term disability, affecting 1.3 billion people across 83 countries. The Global Programme to Eliminate Lymphatic Filariasis (GPELF) was established in 2000 to help these countries provide annual, single-dose mass drug administration to at-risk populations. This initiative relies on the long-term commitment of pharmaceutical companies GlaxoSmithKline and Merck & Co., Inc. to donate the necessary medications until the disease is eliminated. The program protects newborns and the general public by interrupting the transmission cycle of the parasite in treated areas. For the 28.7 million people already infected at the time of treatment, the program provides significant benefits by halting the progression of the disease. During its first eight years, the program generated an estimated total economic benefit of US$24.0 billion. Approximately US$21.8 billion of this total comes from direct and indirect savings for the 31.4 million individuals treated. These individual savings include US$1.4 billion in avoided treatment costs and US$20.4 billion in prevented lost wages over the patients' lifetimes. Additionally, national health systems in endemic countries are expected to save US$2.2 billion by reducing the need for patient services related to the disease. Because the essential drugs are donated at no cost, the financial return on investment for governments and donors is exceptionally high. The cost-benefit ratio of this program is comparable to or even better than other major health initiatives, such as malaria control efforts. These findings demonstrate that the program is a highly cost-effective and excellent investment in global health.
Gaucher disease is a common lysosomal storage disorder caused by mutations in the GBA gene. Beyond this condition, GBA mutations are also a major risk factor for developing sporadic Parkinson’s disease. To study these links, researchers engineered a medaka fish model lacking the glucocerebrosidase (GCase) enzyme. Unlike humans or mice with this deficiency who die shortly after birth, these fish survive for months, allowing for a detailed study of disease progression.

The GBA-deficient medaka displayed symptoms remarkably similar to human neuronopathic Gaucher disease, including progressive neuronal loss and the appearance of "Gaucher-like" cells in the brain. The study found that GCase deficiency causes lysosomal dysfunction, which interferes with the movement of autophagosomes (waste-degrading structures) within nerve fibers. This disrupted transport leads to axonal swellings where alpha-synuclein—a protein linked to Parkinson’s—builds up inside the autophagosomes. Surprisingly, removing alpha-synuclein did not improve the fish's lifespan or reduce brain inflammation. This suggests that while alpha-synuclein accumulation is a key feature, other pathological factors may drive the primary symptoms of neuronopathic Gaucher disease.
