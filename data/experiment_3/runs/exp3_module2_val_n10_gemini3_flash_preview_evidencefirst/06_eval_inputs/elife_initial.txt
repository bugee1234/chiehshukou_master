Cell-fate reprogramming is a fundamental process in biological development where one specific cell type is converted into another. Researchers used C. elegans, a species of roundworm, as the model organism for this study. In the C. elegans germline, converting germ cells into somatic cells requires the disruption of chromatin, which is the material that packages DNA.
Klebsiella pneumoniae is a significant clinical pathogen that causes serious infections in the lungs, blood, liver, and bladder. This bacterium is a leading cause of hospital-associated infections and is becoming increasingly difficult to treat due to the rise of antibiotic-resistant strains. Neutrophils are the primary immune cells that act as first responders to fight against invading pathogens like Klebsiella pneumoniae. Researchers used mice lacking the Skap2 gene to investigate how the SKAP2 protein influences the immune system's response to bacterial infection. The study utilized a specific strain of Klebsiella pneumoniae to model lung infections and measure how well the mice could control the bacteria. To understand the underlying mechanisms, the team measured the production of reactive oxygen species, which are chemicals neutrophils use to kill bacteria. The study found that the adaptor protein SKAP2 is necessary for the immune system to protect against Klebsiella pneumoniae lung infections. Mice lacking the SKAP2 protein had a 100-fold higher bacterial burden in their lungs compared to normal mice. Experiments showed that neutrophils cannot effectively control the infection if they lack the SKAP2 protein. Specifically, neutrophils lacking SKAP2 fail to produce protective reactive oxygen species in response to Klebsiella pneumoniae. SKAP2 is required to trigger the internal signaling cascade involving enzymes like SFKs and Syk that leads to the release of these germ-killing chemicals. The inability of Skap2-deficient mice to control the infection appears to be caused by these specific defects in neutrophil function at the site of infection. These findings indicate that SKAP2 expression in immune cells derived from bone marrow is essential for containing bacterial growth during lung infections. While these results clarify the immune response in mice, it is currently unknown if SKAP2 plays the same role in the human response to Klebsiella pneumoniae.
Plasmodium vivax is the most geographically widespread species of malaria, causing between 80 and 300 million clinical cases annually. Unlike other forms of malaria, P. vivax can hide in the liver as dormant parasites called hypnozoites, making it harder to control than Plasmodium falciparum. These hypnozoites act as a third parasite reservoir alongside the blood and mosquito reservoirs found in other malaria species. Relapses occur when these dormant liver-stage parasites activate and cause new blood-stage infections weeks or months after the initial mosquito bite. Standard malaria models for P. falciparum are not applicable to P. vivax because they fail to account for this liver reservoir. Researchers used a mathematical model to simulate the transmission of P. vivax between humans and mosquitoes by accounting for both new bites and relapses. The model assumes that hypnozoites in the liver can either activate to cause a relapse or die naturally. In the model, the number of hypnozoites in the liver increases with new infections and decreases when they activate or die. For temperate strains of the parasite, the model includes a long-latency phase where hypnozoites remain dormant for approximately six months before they can activate. The distribution of these dormant parasites is uneven across a population, meaning some individuals carry many parasites while others carry none. A higher number of hypnozoites in the liver is associated with more frequent relapses and a shorter time until the first relapse occurs. Individuals with a higher number of hypnozoites are predicted to contribute more to the spread of P. vivax because they relapse more often. Because a single mosquito bite can cause multiple blood-stage infections through relapses, P. vivax is more difficult to eliminate than P. falciparum. Standard anti-malarial drugs target the parasite in the blood but do not directly target the liver-stage hypnozoites. Primaquine is a specific drug that can eliminate P. vivax hypnozoites from the liver. Including primaquine in first-line malaria treatments is predicted to significantly reduce both blood-stage and liver-stage infections. Using drugs like primaquine is predicted to significantly reduce transmission because it targets the individuals most likely to relapse and spread the disease. Treating P. vivax with a combination of blood-stage anti-malarials and primaquine can clear both active blood infections and dormant liver-stage parasites.
Unlike land that is cleared for farming or ranching, forests that are selectively logged for specific trees remain as forested areas and have the potential to regain their stored carbon over time. The recovery of this carbon in logged Amazonian forests happens through two main processes: the continued growth of trees that survived the logging and the arrival of new young trees. Researchers used advanced statistical tools called Bayesian hierarchical models to estimate how these carbon stocks change across the Amazon basin after logging disturbances. The study specifically modeled the recovery of carbon by distinguishing between the growth of surviving trees and the recruitment of new ones. To understand what drives these changes, the researchers investigated how factors like logging intensity, initial forest carbon levels, rainfall, and soil density influence the recovery process. Because many of the study sites had been monitored for less than a decade, the researchers focused their basin-wide predictions on the first 10 years following a disturbance. Across the entire Amazon region, trees that survive the initial logging are the primary contributors to the recovery of carbon stocks. Immediately after logging, the recruitment of new trees is high because there is less competition for sunlight and resources, though this rate slows down as the forest fills back in. The speed at which these surviving trees recover carbon is heavily influenced by how intense the initial logging disturbance was. Carbon recovery over 10 years is significantly higher in the Guiana Shield and western Amazon than in the southern Amazon. In the Guiana Shield and northwestern Amazonia, the high levels of recovery are driven specifically by the robust growth of the surviving trees. In contrast, the southern Amazon shows the lowest rates of carbon recovery compared to other regions. High environmental stress in the south, characterized by low rainfall and high seasonality, limits the ability of these forests to bounce back. Specifically, high seasonal water stress in the southern region appears to be the main constraint that slows down the growth of surviving trees. Environmental factors such as annual rainfall, seasonal water stress, and soil density are critical in determining how quickly different forest plots recover. Because many commercial tree species grow slowly, future timber stocks in the Amazon will depend almost entirely on the trees that survive the first round of logging. Ultimately, surviving trees play the most vital role in ensuring that disturbed Amazonian forests can continue to function and store carbon.
Researchers have historically used the formation of 'neurospheres' in laboratory dishes as a proxy for neural stem cell function, though it has remained unclear if the specific cells starting these spheres are the actual stem cells. Neural stem cells are found in two specific areas of the adult mammalian brain, including the subventricular zone and the subgranular zone. In the living brain, these neural stem cells are typically long-lived and exist in a quiescent, or inactive, state. These stem cells persist throughout adulthood and are responsible for producing neurons, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes. To better understand these processes, researchers isolated cells from the subventricular zone of adult mice between 8 and 60 weeks of age. The study utilized flow cytometry to sort these brain cells into different groups based on specific surface markers to identify and measure the frequency of neural stem cells. Researchers identified 17 cell surface markers that can be used to enrich and isolate neural-initiating cells from the adult mouse subventricular zone. Isolated brain cells were then tested for their ability to form neurospheres, which measures a stem cell's ability to self-renew and differentiate into different cell types. The researchers identified two distinct populations of cells in the adult mouse brain's subventricular zone: GEPCOT cells and pre-GEPCOT cells. GEPCOT cells were found to be highly active and divide frequently in the living brain, with nearly all cells dividing within a 24-hour period. While GEPCOT cells were highly enriched for the ability to form neurospheres in culture, they were found to be short-lived in the living organism. In contrast, pre-GEPCOT cells were found to be highly quiescent and do not divide frequently in the living brain. Unlike the GEPCOT cells, these pre-GEPCOT cells were unable to form neurospheres or colonies when placed in laboratory cultures. The study identified pre-GEPCOT cells as the true quiescent neural stem cells because they are long-lived and survive chemotherapy-like treatments that ablate other cells. These pre-GEPCOT cells act as a precursor population that replenishes the more active GEPCOT neural intermediate cells. The ability to isolate these specific cell types using surface markers allows scientists to study how neural stem cells behave in a living organism rather than relying on artificial laboratory cultures. This discovery distinguishes quiescent neural stem cells from the cells that initiate neurospheres, making it possible to study their unique properties in vivo.
