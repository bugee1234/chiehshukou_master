Gene expression levels vary significantly between individuals, and these differences can influence traits related to evolution and disease. The role of RNA 3′-end processing in gene expression variation is not well understood compared to other regulatory mechanisms. Researchers developed a high-throughput sequencing approach to precisely measure the ends of mRNA transcripts and identify functional regulatory elements. The study used mRNA sequencing to identify genetic variations in the 3′ untranslated regions of genes across six individuals to see how they affect expression levels. Researchers found that transcript end positions in human cells are not random but are located near specific regulatory elements, such as microRNA sites. Natural genetic variations in the polyadenylation signal can cause genes to produce different lengths of 3′ transcript forms. The choice between short and long forms of a gene's 3′ end determines which regulatory elements are included in the final mature mRNA. Genetic variants in specific 3′ regulatory motifs, such as AREs and Pumilio sites, are significant predictors of how much a gene is expressed in different individuals. Genetic variants can reduce total gene expression by forcing the production of transcripts that are processed less efficiently or are less stable. Sequence differences in polyadenylation signals are a key mechanism explaining why gene expression levels vary between individual humans. Genetic differences in how RNA ends are processed can influence the risk of both rare Mendelian diseases and common complex human diseases. Models of gene expression variation are likely incomplete if they only consider transcription initiation and splicing without accounting for 3′-end processing.
The live attenuated simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV) vaccine, known as SIVΔnef, is highly effective at inducing protection against wild-type lentiviral infections. Researchers used next-generation sequencing to examine how the virus evolved in 12 rhesus macaques after they were vaccinated with this weakened version of the virus. The study found that the SIVΔnef vaccine undergoes sequence evolution and viral escape during the vaccination period despite low levels of viral replication. In vaccinated animals, the CD8 T cell immune response shifts its focus to different parts of the virus between 5 and 20 weeks after vaccination. This shift causes the immune system to focus more on Gag and Pol, which are the most stable and conserved proteins of the virus. While immune responses targeting variable regions tend to decline as the virus mutates, responses targeting stable, conserved regions are maintained over time. The vaccine also allows the immune system to generate new responses to viral variants that have escaped previous immune detection, a process termed 'deepening' the immune response. Increased vaccine protection correlates with this broader and deeper CD8 T cell repertoire that targets stable parts of the virus. These findings suggest that a successful HIV vaccine should focus the immune system's T cells on conserved regions of the virus and their known variants. Future vaccine designs should choose components that preferentially target these stable regions to increase the breadth and depth of the immune response simultaneously.
When the fungus Candida albicans invades the tongue, the body must rapidly recruit white blood cells called neutrophils to the oral mucosa to prevent the infection from spreading. While the IL-17 signaling pathway is a known part of fungal defense, researchers found that the interleukin-1 receptor (IL-1R) pathway is actually the primary controller of neutrophil movement during acute oral infections. Using a mouse model of oral candidiasis, scientists investigated how the IL-1 pathway coordinates the immune response by measuring gene expression and studying cell-to-cell communication. The study found that when live, invading fungal hyphae damage tongue cells called keratinocytes, these cells release a signaling molecule called IL-1α as an 'alarmin' to alert the host. This IL-1α signal acts on nearby blood vessel cells, known as endothelial cells, which respond by producing a growth factor called G-CSF. G-CSF then enters the bloodstream to reach the bone marrow, where it triggers 'emergency granulopoiesis' to produce and release a fresh supply of neutrophils. Simultaneously, IL-1R signaling helps recruit existing neutrophils from the blood by promoting the production of chemical attractants in the infected tissue. These findings reveal a critical communication loop between skin cells and blood vessel cells that ensures the body meets the high demand for immune cells during a fungal attack. Understanding this IL-1-dependent process is vital, as G-CSF is already used therapeutically to treat patients with low white blood cell counts.
Lymphatic filariasis is a parasitic infection that stands as a leading cause of permanent and long-term disability, affecting 1.3 billion people across 83 countries. The Global Programme to Eliminate Lymphatic Filariasis (GPELF) was established in 2000 to help these countries provide annual, single-dose mass drug administration to at-risk populations. This initiative relies on the long-term commitment of pharmaceutical companies GlaxoSmithKline and Merck & Co., Inc. to donate the necessary medications until the disease is eliminated. The program protects newborns and the general public by interrupting the transmission cycle of the parasite in treated areas. For the 28.7 million people already infected at the time of treatment, the program provides significant benefits by halting the progression of the disease. During its first eight years, the program generated an estimated total economic benefit of US$24.0 billion. Approximately US$21.8 billion of this total comes from direct and indirect savings for the 31.4 million individuals treated. These individual savings include US$1.4 billion in avoided treatment costs and US$20.4 billion in prevented lost wages over the patients' lifetimes. Additionally, national health systems in endemic countries are expected to save US$2.2 billion by reducing the need for patient services related to the disease. Because the essential drugs are donated at no cost, the financial return on investment for governments and donors is exceptionally high. The cost-benefit ratio of this program is comparable to or even better than other major health initiatives, such as malaria control efforts. These findings demonstrate that the program is a highly cost-effective and excellent investment in global health.
Gaucher disease is the most common lysosomal storage disorder and is caused by mutations in both copies of the glucocerebrosidase (GBA) gene. Recent genetic research has also identified GBA mutations as a significant risk factor for developing sporadic Parkinson’s disease. To study these conditions, researchers developed a specific medaka fish mutant that lacks the GBA gene. Unlike human and mouse models with this deficiency that die shortly after birth, these medaka fish survive for more than three months. This extended lifespan allows scientists to observe the long-term progression of the disease's pathology. The GBA-deficient fish displayed symptoms similar to human Gaucher disease, including the loss of nerve cells and the presence of specialized Gaucher-like cells in the brain. The study found that alpha-synuclein, a protein closely linked to Parkinson's disease, accumulated within the brains of these fish. This protein buildup occurred inside autophagosomes, which are cellular structures responsible for degrading waste. The researchers discovered that the lack of the GBA enzyme causes lysosomal dysfunction, which disrupts the transport of these waste-processing structures and leads to swelling in nerve fibers. Surprisingly, removing the alpha-synuclein protein did not improve the fish's lifespan, movement, or brain inflammation. These findings suggest that GBA-deficient medaka are a valuable model for understanding how Gaucher disease and related neurological issues develop over time.
