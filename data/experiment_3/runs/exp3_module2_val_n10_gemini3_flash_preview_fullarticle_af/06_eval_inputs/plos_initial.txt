Gene expression levels vary significantly between individuals, and these differences can influence traits related to evolution and disease. (E001) While researchers understand how DNA changes upstream of genes affect expression, the role of RNA 3′-end processing—the mechanism that determines where a transcript stops—remains less understood. (E002, E017) This process, known as alternative polyadenylation, determines the length of the 3′ untranslated region, which controls a transcript's stability, translation, and location within a cell. (E006, E011) To investigate this, researchers developed a high-throughput sequencing approach to precisely measure the ends of mRNA transcripts and identify functional regulatory elements across six human cell lines. (E012, E018, E019) The study analyzed whether genetic variations in polyadenylation signals and other 3′ motifs are associated with changes in total gene expression levels. (E020, E025) The researchers found that transcript end positions are not random but are located near specific regulatory sites, such as microRNA binding regions. (E003) Genetic differences between individuals contribute to variations in 3′-end processing, particularly in immune-related genes. (E022, E023) Variations in polyadenylation signals can force the production of transcripts that are less stable or processed less efficiently, leading to lower total gene expression. (E007, E024) The choice between short and long transcript forms determines which regulatory elements, such as Pumilio sites, are included in the final mature mRNA. (E009, E010, E013, E021) These sequence differences in polyadenylation signals are a key mechanism explaining why gene expression levels vary between individual humans. (E004, E008, E014) Because these variations can influence the risk of both rare and common diseases, models of gene expression are incomplete if they do not account for 3′-end processing. (E005, E015) A complete understanding of human genetics requires integrating these processing signals with the sequence elements that control the ultimate fate of the transcript. (E016)
The live attenuated simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV) vaccine, known as SIVΔnef, is highly effective at protecting against wild-type lentiviral challenges, including mucosal and heterologous viral strains. Researchers used next-generation sequencing to examine how the virus evolved in 12 rhesus macaques after they were vaccinated with this weakened version of the virus. The study found that the SIVΔnef vaccine undergoes sequence evolution and viral escape during the vaccination period, even when viral replication levels are low. The virus frequently develops mutations to escape the immune system's CD8 T cell responses, particularly in highly variable regions of its genome. Between 5 and 20 weeks after vaccination, the CD8 T cell immune response shifts its focus to different parts of the virus. This process, called anentropic specificity, causes the immune response to shift toward targeting highly conserved and stable viral regions, such as the Gag and Pol proteins. By 20 weeks post-vaccination, the immune system focuses more on these stable parts rather than regions that change easily, regardless of the animal's genetic background. While immune responses targeting variable regions decline as the virus mutates, responses targeting stable regions are maintained over time. The vaccine also generates new, de novo immune responses to viral variants that have escaped previous detection, a process termed 'deepening' of the immune repertoire. SIVΔnef acts as a persistent vaccine that evolves alongside the immune response, helping to shape and mature the body's defenses. Researchers hypothesize that the efficacy of live-attenuated vaccines depends on this viral evolution to redirect immune targeting toward conserved regions. Increased vaccine protection correlates with this broader and deeper CD8 T cell repertoire that targets stable parts of the virus. These findings provide a model for successful HIV vaccine design, suggesting that future vaccines should focus T cells on conserved viral regions and their known variants.
Mucosal infections caused by the fungus Candida albicans are common, but the mechanisms the body uses to trigger antifungal immunity are not fully understood. Researchers used a mouse model of oral candidiasis to investigate how the immune system recruits neutrophils, which are white blood cells essential for controlling fungal growth and spread. While the IL-17 pathway is known to be important for fungal defense, this study found that the interleukin-1 receptor (IL-1R) pathway is the primary controller of neutrophil movement to the oral mucosa. The study utilized tongue-derived keratinocytes and blood endothelial cell lines to observe how different cell types communicate during an infection. Researchers also measured the expression of genes for inflammatory signals, such as IL-1α and IL-1β, and neutrophil-recruiting chemokines in tongue tissues. The results showed that IL-1R signaling is essential for recruiting existing neutrophils from the blood and triggering the production of new ones in the bone marrow. When live, invading fungal hyphae damage oral keratinocytes, these cells release IL-1α as an 'alarmin' to alert the host. This IL-1α signal prompts nearby blood endothelial cells to produce G-CSF, a growth factor that enters the bloodstream to reach the bone marrow. G-CSF then triggers 'emergency granulopoiesis,' a process that boosts the production and mobilization of neutrophils to meet the high demand caused by the infection. These findings demonstrate that a coordinated crosstalk between keratinocytes and endothelial cells is vital for protecting the body from Candida albicans.
Lymphatic filariasis is a parasitic infection that stands as a primary cause of permanent and long-term disability across the globe. To combat this, the Global Programme to Eliminate Lymphatic Filariasis (GPELF) was established in 2000 to provide annual mass drug administration to populations at risk. The program relies on long-term drug donations from pharmaceutical companies GlaxoSmithKline and Merck & Co., Inc., which are committed to providing these treatments until the disease is eliminated. By interrupting the transmission cycle, the program protects newborns and the general population from ever acquiring the infection. For those already infected, the treatment provides significant health benefits by halting the progression of the disease. During its first eight years, the GPELF generated an estimated total economic benefit of US$24.0 billion. This total includes US$21.8 billion in direct and indirect savings for treated individuals, consisting of US$1.4 billion in avoided treatment costs and US$20.4 billion in prevented lost wages. Additionally, national health systems in endemic countries saved approximately US$2.2 billion by reducing the need for patient services. Because essential drugs are donated at no cost, the financial return on investment for governments and donors is exceptionally high. The cost-benefit ratio of this program is comparable to or even better than other major global health initiatives, such as malaria control programs.
Gaucher disease is the most common lysosomal storage disorder and is caused by having two mutated copies of the GBA gene. While these mutations cause Gaucher disease, carrying even a single mutated GBA gene is a significant risk factor for developing sporadic Parkinson’s disease. To study this connection, researchers created a specific mutant medaka fish (GBAW337X/W337X) that lacks the glucocerebrosidase enzyme. Unlike human and mouse models with this deficiency that die shortly after birth, these medaka fish survive for more than three months. This extended lifespan allowed researchers to use the fish brains to study the progression of Gaucher disease and the expression of proteins like alpha-synuclein and APOE. The GBA-deficient medaka displayed symptoms similar to human neuronopathic Gaucher disease, including the loss of nerve cells and the presence of specialized "Gaucher-like" cells in the brain. The study found that the enzyme deficiency causes lysosomal dysfunction, which leads to the accumulation of waste materials and dysfunctional mitochondria. This dysfunction disrupts the transport of waste-degrading structures called autophagosomes, causing them to swell within nerve fibers. Alpha-synuclein, a protein closely linked to Parkinson's disease, was found to accumulate within these swollen autophagosomes in the fish brains. Surprisingly, removing the alpha-synuclein protein did not improve the fish's lifespan, movement, brain inflammation, or nerve cell loss. These findings establish GBA-deficient medaka as a viable model for studying how Gaucher disease progresses and how alpha-synuclein accumulation occurs in the brain.
