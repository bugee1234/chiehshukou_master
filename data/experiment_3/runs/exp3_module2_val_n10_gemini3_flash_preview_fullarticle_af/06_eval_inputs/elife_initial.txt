Cell-fate reprogramming is a fundamental process in biological development where one cell type is converted into another. Researchers used C. elegans, a species of roundworm, as the model organism for this study. In the C. elegans germline, converting germ cells into somatic cells requires the disruption of chromatin, which is the material that packages DNA.
Klebsiella pneumoniae is a major clinical pathogen that causes serious infections in the lungs, blood, liver, and bladder. This bacterium is a leading cause of infections acquired in hospitals and is increasingly difficult to treat because many strains have developed resistance to antibiotics. Neutrophils are the primary immune cells that act as first responders to fight off invading pathogens like K. pneumoniae. Researchers used a specific strain of K. pneumoniae to study how lung infections progress in mice that were genetically modified to lack a protein called SKAP2. The team measured the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS)—chemicals used by immune cells to kill bacteria—to determine if SKAP2 is necessary for this protective response. The study found that SKAP2 is essential for the immune system to protect the lungs, as mice lacking this protein had a 100-fold higher bacterial burden and were less able to control the infection. This failure to control the bacteria depends specifically on the absence of SKAP2 in immune cells derived from the bone marrow. While neutrophils were present at the site of infection, they could not effectively contain the bacteria without the SKAP2 protein. Specifically, neutrophils lacking SKAP2 failed to produce protective ROS when they encountered K. pneumoniae or when their surface receptors were stimulated. SKAP2 works by interacting with enzymes like Src family kinases to activate the signaling circuit required for ROS production. These findings suggest that the increased severity of lung infections in SKAP2-deficient mice is directly linked to the inability of their neutrophils to generate these germ-killing chemicals. While these results clarify how the immune system fights K. pneumoniae in mice, it is not yet known if SKAP2 plays the same critical role in the human immune response.
Plasmodium vivax is the most geographically widespread species of malaria, causing between 80 and 300 million clinical cases annually. A defining feature of this parasite is its ability to hide in the liver as dormant parasites called hypnozoites, which can activate weeks or months after the initial infection to cause new blood-stage infections known as relapses. These hypnozoites constitute a third parasite reservoir alongside the blood and mosquito reservoirs found in other malaria species like Plasmodium falciparum. Because standard malaria models do not account for this liver reservoir, they are not applicable to P. vivax. Researchers developed a mathematical model to simulate the transmission of P. vivax between humans and mosquitoes by accounting for the liver-stage reservoir. The model assumes that the number of hypnozoites in the liver increases with new mosquito bites and decreases when the parasites either activate to cause a relapse or die naturally. The researchers used individual patient data to estimate the time to the first relapse, though they had to make certain assumptions because the data were not sufficient to estimate all parameters simultaneously. The model predicts that the distribution of dormant liver parasites is highly uneven across a population, with some individuals carrying many parasites while others carry none. Individuals with a higher number of hypnozoites are predicted to experience more frequent relapses and a shorter time until their first relapse occurs. The model also suggests that relapses can occur immediately after the initial infection because hypnozoites activate at a constant rate. In temperate regions, the parasites exhibit a long-latency phase where they remain dormant for approximately six months before they are capable of activating. P. vivax is more difficult to control than P. falciparum because a single mosquito bite can cause multiple blood-stage infections through these relapses. Standard anti-malarial drugs like chloroquine target the blood reservoir but do not directly affect the liver-stage hypnozoites. The drug primaquine can clear both active blood infections and dormant liver-stage parasites. Including primaquine in first-line treatments is predicted to significantly reduce transmission because it targets the individuals most likely to relapse.
Selective logging in the Amazon removes specific trees for timber, but unlike land cleared for farming, these forests remain standing and can eventually regain their lost carbon. This recovery process occurs through two main pathways: the continued growth of trees that survived the logging and the recruitment of new young trees. To understand how these forests bounce back, researchers used Bayesian hierarchical models to estimate changes in aboveground carbon stocks across the Amazon basin. The study specifically distinguished between the contributions of surviving trees and new recruits to model recovery trajectories. The researchers investigated how recovery is influenced by factors such as the intensity of the logging, the initial amount of carbon in the forest, annual rainfall, and soil density. Due to the length of available data from various experimental sites, the team focused their regional predictions on the first 10 years following the disturbance. The results show that trees surviving the logging are the primary drivers of carbon recovery across the entire Amazon. Immediately after logging, the recruitment of new trees increases significantly because there is less competition for sunlight and nutrients. Carbon recovery is highest in the Guiana Shield and western Amazon, where surviving trees grow back more vigorously. In contrast, the southern Amazon shows the lowest recovery rates because high environmental stress, including low rainfall and long dry seasons, limits tree growth. The speed of this recovery is also heavily dictated by the initial logging intensity and local environmental conditions like soil bulk density. Because many valuable timber species grow slowly, future wood stocks will depend almost entirely on the health and survival of the trees left behind after the first harvest.
Neural stem cells (NSCs) are found in two specific areas of the adult mammalian brain: the subgranular zone and the subventricular zone (SVZ). In the living brain, these stem cells persist throughout adulthood and produce essential brain cells, including neurons, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes. Researchers have historically used 'neurosphere' formation in a lab dish as a proxy for neural stem cell function, but it has remained unclear if the cells that start these spheres are the actual long-lived stem cells. To investigate this, researchers isolated cells from the SVZ of adult mice and used flow cytometry to sort them into different groups based on 17 specific surface markers. The study utilized a combination of these markers to isolate and test two specific cell populations named GEPCOT and pre-GEPCOT cells. Isolated brain cells were tested for their ability to form neurospheres to measure their capacity to self-renew and differentiate into different cell types. The researchers identified two distinct populations in the SVZ: GEPCOT cells, which have high levels of EGFR and PlexinB2 proteins, and pre-GEPCOT cells. GEPCOT cells are highly active and divide frequently in the living brain, with nearly all cells dividing within a 24-hour period. However, these GEPCOT cells are short-lived and only persist transiently before being replaced. In contrast, pre-GEPCOT cells are quiescent, meaning they are dormant and do not divide frequently in the living brain. Unlike GEPCOT cells, pre-GEPCOT cells are unable to form neurospheres in laboratory cultures but are long-lived and survive chemotherapy-like treatments. Genetic markers and analysis of the SVZ and olfactory bulb confirmed that these quiescent pre-GEPCOT cells act as the true long-lived stem cells that replenish the active GEPCOT population. The ability to isolate these specific cell types using surface markers allows scientists to study how neural stem cells behave directly in living tissue rather than relying on artificial laboratory cultures.
